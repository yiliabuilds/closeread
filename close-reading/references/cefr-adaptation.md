# CEFR Depth Adaptation

One rule set, five depth profiles. This file answers **how deep** — never **which phenomena**.
Phenomena belong to `languages/<lang>.md`. Keeping that split is what lets German B1 and English B1
share a depth profile while analysing entirely different grammar.

All numbers below are **calibration targets, not quotas**. Overshooting a target is a worse error
than undershooting it.

---

## 1. The master table

Assume a standard article of 500–800 words unless noted. For short or long texts, see §4.

| Dimension | A1–A2 | B1 | B2 | C1 | C2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Focus sentences | 2–3 | 3–4 | 4–5 | 4–6 | 4–6 |
| Expanded modules (beyond map, focus sentences, practice) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 3 |
| Selection threshold | must be comprehension-critical **and** error-prone | + transferable | + argument-critical | + stylistically marked | + interpretively contested |
| Global read length | 3–5 short lines | 5–8 lines | 8–12 lines | 10–15 lines | 10–15 lines |
| Metalanguage | near zero: subject / verb / "这一部分修饰…" | minimal: main clause, subordinate clause, modifier | moderate: participle, appositive, concession, scope | full, used economically | full, but assume control |
| Inline glosses in L1 | freely, for any word that blocks | only for context-shifted items | rare | none unless asked | none |
| Recognition items | 4–5 | 5–6 | 5–8 | 4–6 | 3–5 |
| Production items | 2–3 | 3–4 | 3–5 | 3–4 | 2–3 |
| Architecture depth | sequence only: what each part does | + one hinge, 1–2 paragraph-level moves | + move-level observations | + how sequencing creates effect | + alternative architectures the author rejected |
| Stance & rhetoric | tone in one word | brief, inside another section | hedging, concession, modality | + distancing, qualification, register | + irony, echo, allusion, argumentative framing |
| Transfer patterns | 1–2, sentence-level | 2–3, at most one discourse frame | 3–4, incl. one discourse frame | 3–4, discourse-level preferred | 2–3, high-precision only |
| Practice tasks | 2, concrete | 2–3 | 3 | 3–4 | 2–3, harder |
| Output length (chars, CN explanation) | 300–500 | 500–700 | 700–1000 | 800–1200 | 800–1200 |

**A1 and A2 share one column.** All six bands are supported, but five depth profiles are maintained.
A1 is served at the low end of each A1–A2 range rather than from a profile of its own; the two bands
differ less in what to explain than in how much scaffolding to give, and that difference is already
carried by the range.

**A band sets priority, not permission.** A column describes where attention goes by default, and how
much output the reader should have to absorb. It does not forbid an insight from a higher column. An
advanced observation may cross bands when it is needed to understand the text, when it can be said
without new terminology, and when it does not displace something more basic. What must not happen is
an insight arriving as an equal-weight section that competes with the basics for attention — that is
a priority failure, not a level failure.

The counter-intuitive entries are deliberate: **C1 and C2 get fewer vocabulary items and fewer
practice tasks than B2.** Advanced learners gain more from precision on a few items than from
volume, and their remaining gaps are not lexical quantity.

## 2. Profile notes

### A1–A2 — comprehension first (shared profile)

Goal: the learner finishes knowing what the text says and how its sentences are put together at the
simplest level. Nothing else.

- Analyse sentences only where a learner would genuinely mis-parse them. At this level that usually
  means: negation scope, question formation, `there is`, basic tense contrast, prepositional
  attachment.
- State the backbone explicitly and in ordinary words. A bracketed skeleton helps.
- Explain connectors as *meanings*, not categories: "but 表示前后相反".
- Do not analyse rhetoric. Do not discuss authorial stance beyond "作者是赞成的 / 反对的".
- Allowed: shorter quotes, more L1 support, simpler English in any English explanation.
- Not default at this level: participle clauses, nominalisation, register. Any of these may appear if
  a sentence cannot be read without it, but then it is a rescue, not a module.

### B1 — from sentences to paragraphs

The pivot level. The learner can handle the sentence; what they cannot yet do is see how sentences
combine into a move, and act on what they have read. B1 output makes structure visible and leaves
the reader with something operable — it is not a survey of everything the text does.

Aims, in priority order:

1. Understand the whole — the spine, not a summary.
2. See how the hard sentences are built, well enough to re-read them unaided.
3. Take away a small set of collocations and expressions that are genuinely reusable. At this level
   the interesting vocabulary is usually a *known* word behaving unusually, not an unknown one.
4. Begin to see paragraph-level development: how a paragraph holds together (reference,
   substitution, connector chains) and how one paragraph sets up the next.
5. Build the habit of moving from sentence to paragraph instead of stopping at the sentence.

**Density discipline.** The failure mode at B1 is not shallowness, it is accumulation — every module
is individually affordable, so all of them get expanded, and the reader cannot tell what mattered.

- Expand three modules beyond the map, the focus sentences, and practice. Choose by what this reader
  will use, not by what the text affords.
- Prefer **fewer modules with clear priorities** over every module with shortened text. A brief
  version of all six is worse than three done properly.
- Anything already explained in a focus sentence gets no second section. Reference it in half a line
  or drop it. Restating is not reinforcing (see `core-method.md` §9).

**Higher-band material is allowed here.** A concession strategy, a hedging marker, a scope limit, a
discourse frame — admit any of them when it is needed to understand the text, can be said without
new terminology, and does not push out something more basic. Deliver it as a sentence or two
*inside* a section that already exists, not as a fifth section with its own heading. The test is
position and weight, not whether the insight "belongs to B2".

Do not manufacture simplicity. If the text's central move is a concession, B1 needs to see the
concession — pretending it is not there is not calibration.

### B2 — argument and stance take the centre

The level where CloseRead's distinctive value appears.

- Argument-critical sentences now qualify for analysis on their own.
- Stance moves from a side remark to a section of its own: hedging, concession, modality,
  attribution. Use the hedge ladder in `core-method.md` §6 — but only the layers the text uses.
- Architecture: move-level observations, not just sequence. "Why is the concession placed first?"
- Transfer moves up a level: at least one discourse frame, not only sentence patterns.
- Reduce basic grammar explanation sharply. If a B2 learner needs the passive explained, they are
  not B2 for this text — recalibrate (§5), do not silently teach down.

### C1 — precision, not coverage

- Fewer items, deeper. A C1 output with eight vocabulary entries is a mis-calibrated output.
- Prioritise: lexical precision (why *this* word and not its near-synonym), qualification and
  distancing, how the author manages the reader's inference.
- Grammar commentary should be about *choice*, not *structure*: "为什么这里用倒装" rather than
  "这是倒装句".
- Architecture analysis can include what the author withheld or sequenced to create effect.
- Assume the learner knows the words. Explain why the words are arranged that way.

### C2 — interpretation and style

- The learning value is in contested readings, stylistic control, and argumentative framing.
- Vocabulary only where nuance is genuinely at stake; never for coverage.
- Invite disagreement: where a reading is arguable, say so and give the competing reading.
- Practice should be productive at a high level: imitation of a stylistic move, rewriting a passage
  in a different register, reconstructing a suppressed counter-argument.
- Do not explain anything the learner can see. State the observation and move to its effect.

### B1 vs C1 — emphasis, not capability

The two bands can notice the same things. What differs is what the output spends its attention on.
Use this to keep B1 from becoming a dumbed-down C1, and C1 from becoming a padded B1.

| Same text, different question | B1 | C1 |
|---|---|---|
| A hard sentence | How do I read it on my own next time? | Why was it built this way rather than another way? |
| A word worth keeping | What do I use it with, and when? | Why this word and not its near-synonym? |
| The structure | Where does the text turn? | What does the sequencing buy the argument? |
| The author's stance | Noted in a line, if it matters | The main event |
| The reader's takeaway | Something to re-use tomorrow | A judgement about how the piece works |

## 3. What to stop explaining (cumulative)

Read as a staircase — each level adds to the list below it.

| From | Stop explaining |
|---|---|
| B1 | basic tense formation, plural rules, articles in simple cases, "this is a noun" |
| B2 | passive formation, standard relative clauses, common conditionals, basic connectors |
| C1 | any structure the learner can parse on first read; word meanings that are not context-shifted |
| C2 | nearly all structure; most lexis; anything that reads as a textbook entry |

If a draft violates this table, the level estimate is probably wrong — fix the estimate rather than
padding the analysis.

## 4. Text length adjustment

| Text length | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Under ~150 words (a paragraph, a stanza) | Treat the whole text as the unit. Focus sentences: at most half the sentence count. Global read compresses to 1–2 lines; architecture may merge with the global read. |
| 500–800 words | Master table as written. |
| 800–2000 words | Focus sentences stay at the ceiling (≤6). Selection tightens: prefer hinge sentences and the argument spine. Architecture gets proportionally more space. |
| Over 2000 words, or multiple sections | Do not attempt full coverage. Read the opening, the hinge, and the close; then offer to continue section by section. Say explicitly that the middle was skimmed for selection purposes. |

## 5. Recalibration mid-conversation

The first estimate is a guess. Treat user signals as data and adjust without ceremony.

| Signal | Action |
|---|---|
| "太难了" / "看不懂" / asks what a basic word means | Drop one level. Re-explain the current sentence with more L1, less metalanguage. Do not restart the whole output. |
| "太基础了" / "这些我都知道" / "skip the basics" | Raise one level. Abandon the vocabulary section, move to stance and architecture. |
| "还是不懂这句" (second time on the same sentence) | The failure is structural, not lexical. Re-do the backbone with a bracketed skeleton and a literal word-order reading, then the natural reading. |
| User answers a practice task correctly and easily | Raise the difficulty of the next task, not the amount. |
| User's own writing in the conversation shows clear control | Trust the writing over the stated level. |

Never announce the recalibration as a correction ("抱歉我高估了你的水平"). Just adjust.

## 6. When the level is unknown

Order of inference:

1. Explicit statement ("我 B2", "I'm C1").
2. Their own writing in the conversation — sentence complexity, collocation accuracy, error types.
3. Their request phrasing — "长难句" suggests B1–B2; "nuance / register / stance" suggests C1+;
   "看不懂" with simple vocabulary suggests A2–B1.
4. Text difficulty they chose — but weight this *lowest*; learners routinely pick texts above level.
5. Default: **B2**, not B1. B2 is the level at which CloseRead's distinctive modules earn their
   place, and an over-levelled output is easier for a B1 learner to ignore than an under-levelled
   one is for a B2 learner to use.

State the assumption in half a line and move on: "按 B2 处理" — then invite correction only if the
output would otherwise mislead.

Inferring `target_language` and `explanation_language` is a different decision and lives in
`references/input-routing.md` §2. This section decides only how advanced the learner is.
