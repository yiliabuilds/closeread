# English Language Module

What is worth noticing in English, and what Chinese-speaking learners specifically misread.
Depth is **not** specified here — that comes from `cefr-adaptation.md`. This file supplies
phenomena only.

---

## 1. The eight things English close reading actually attends to

Organised by function, not by grammar-label. These are the recurrent sources of learning value in
prose English.

| # | Phenomenon | Why it carries value | Typical surface forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Information packing** | English compresses whole propositions into modifiers; unpacking them is the main reading skill above B1 | participle phrases, reduced relatives, appositives, absolute constructions, premodifier chains ("a widely criticised, poorly funded initiative") |
| 2 | **Nominalisation** | Turns actions into things; creates abstraction, authority, and density — and hides the agent | "the implementation of the policy", "their refusal to negotiate" |
| 3 | **Hedging and boosting** | Carries the author's epistemic stance; the difference between *suggests* and *shows* is the argument | modals, *arguably*, *apparently*, *clearly*, *in fact*, reporting verbs |
| 4 | **Concession architecture** | English argument prefers *concede-then-assert*; the concession's position changes the rhetoric | *while/although/even if/admittedly/it is true that… but/granted* |
| 5 | **End-weight and end-focus** | English pushes long constituents right and puts new/important information last | extraposition (*it is clear that…*), cleft (*what matters is…*), postposed clauses |
| 6 | **Reference chains** | Cohesion via pronouns and demonstratives; failure here is the most common comprehension break | *this / that / it / such / the former*, whole-clause *this* |
| 7 | **Punctuation as syntax** | In English, the dash, colon, and semicolon are structural signals, not decoration | dash aside, colon = elaboration, semicolon = balanced contrast |
| 8 | **Parallelism and tricolon** | Rhythm doing argumentative work; signals enumeration and equivalence | *not only…but also*, three-item lists, repeated frames |

If a passage has none of these, it is probably not a good close-reading text — say so rather than
inventing analysis.

## 2. Phenomena by level band

Not a syllabus — a hint about what is *available* to notice. Use with the depth profile.

**A1–A2 available:** basic negation scope, `there is/are`, question formation, simple past vs
present perfect, prepositional phrases of time and place, `because / but / so`, imperatives,
`going to` vs `will`.

**B1 available:** relative clauses (restrictive vs non-restrictive), first/second conditionals,
passive, reported speech, common phrasal verbs, connector chains (`however`, `therefore`,
`in addition`, `on the other hand`), comparison structures.

**B2 available:** participle clauses, appositives, concession (`although / while / even if /
whereas`), third conditional and mixed conditionals, hedging modals, nominalisation, cleft
sentences, `it`-extraposition, cohesion through substitution and ellipsis.

**C1 available:** absolute constructions, inversion after negatives and `only`, subjunctive after
`suggest/insist/demand`, complex nominal groups, distancing devices (`so-called`, scare quotes,
`allegedly`), register shifts, marked word order, irony and understatement.

**C2 available:** everything above plus stylistic choice as such — why this rhythm, why this
latinate word, why the sentence is deliberately unbalanced, what a mixed metaphor is doing.

## 3. Discourse machinery specific to English

Worth naming explicitly when the text uses it, because it is invisible to learners:

- **Given-new ordering** — old information first, new information stressed at the end. When an
  author violates it, they are doing something.
- **Topic sentences that are not first** — English journalism often withholds the claim for two
  sentences to earn attention.
- **The concessive paragraph opener** — starting a paragraph with `While it is true that…` is a
  reliable signal that the paragraph's real work comes after the comma.
- **Scope narrowing** — `in most cases`, `for the most part`, `with some exceptions` quietly limits
  a sweeping claim. Learners read the claim and miss the limit. This is high-value at B2+.
- **Attribution as distance** — `critics argue`, `it has been suggested` moves a claim off the
  author without denying it.
- **The colon as thesis marker** — in op-ed prose, a colon frequently introduces the actual claim.

## 4. Traps for Chinese-speaking learners

The highest-yield section in this file. When one of these appears in a text, it is almost always
worth a focus sentence — it passes the *error-prone* gate automatically.

| Trap | What the learner does | What is actually happening |
|---|---|---|
| `not … because` | reads as "because … not" | scope ambiguity; usually the negation is on the main clause, not the reason |
| `I don't think / believe …` | expects negation in the subordinate clause | English negates the main verb; the meaning is the subordinate negation |
| `once` | reads as pure time ("当…时") | a temporal starting point with a conditional edge: "only after X becomes normal does Y become possible". Say which force dominates, do not deny the other |
| `while` | reads as "当…的时候" | most often concessive "虽然" in argumentative prose, sometimes contrast, sometimes time — the clause decides, and the temporal reading is not absent |
| `as` | picks one reading and stops | cause, simultaneous time, manner, or comparison — the clause's content decides which dominates; the others are not ruled out |
| `since` | reads as time only | usually causal in academic prose, but genuinely temporal when anchored to a point in time |
| whole-clause `this / which` | hunts for a noun antecedent | often refers to the entire preceding proposition; "This is why…" points at a sentence, not a word |
| semicolon | reads as sentence end, breaks the argument | joins two balanced clauses into one thought; the contrast is the point |
| dash pair | reads as an aside to skip | often carries the author's real evaluation; skipping it loses the stance |
| `it is not until … that` | parses `not until` as "不到…" and loses the main clause | the frame is `not until X that Y` = "直到 X 才 Y" |
| `hardly / scarcely / barely … when` | expects `than` or a normal order | negative-polarity frame with inversion; means "刚一…就" |
| `the last …` | reads as "最后一个" | usually "最不可能的" in evaluative prose |
| `more of a X than a Y` | reads as a quantity comparison | a category judgement: "与其说是 Y，不如说是 X" |
| `no more than` + quantity | treated as the same as `not more than` | "only / as little as" — the speaker's evaluation is part of the meaning, often dismissive: `It took no more than five minutes` |
| `not more than` + quantity | merged with `no more than`, or read as praise of smallness | a plain upper bound, "at most" — no built-in "less than you'd expect" judgement: `It took not more than five minutes` |
| `A is no more X than B` | read as a plain quantity comparison, or as one of the two quantity patterns above | neither of them. A comparative frame with **no fixed rhetoric**. Explain what it does in this passage first — e.g. rejecting both positions — before saying what it can do in general |
| `cannot … too` | reads as prohibition | means "再…也不为过" |
| `few / little` vs `a few / a little` | neutralised | bare forms are negative-leaning ("几乎没有") |
| `suggest / insist / demand + that` | expects indicative | subjunctive: bare verb, no `-s`, no `was` |
| `with + noun + participle` | reads as simple "和…一起" | absolute construction: attendant circumstance or cause |
| `rather than` | reads as "而不是" only | also expresses preference and often governs parallelism — check what is parallel to what |
| countability shifts | `research`, `evidence`, `information`, `advice` counted | mass nouns in English; `a research` is an error and a misreading of the source |
| `the` + singular noun | read as specific reference | generic reference: "The tiger is a solitary animal" = tigers in general |
| `would` in past narrative | read as conditional | habitual past: "In those years he would walk to work" |
| fronted negation + inversion | main clause missed | `Never had she…`, `Only then did he…` — the subject follows the auxiliary |

Rule of use: when a text contains one of these, **do not label it as a grammar point.** Explain
the misreading it invites, then the correct parse. Chinese learners do not need the category name;
they need the moment of "啊，我读反了".

Two disciplines apply to every row: name the force that dominates *in this sentence* rather than
denying the others, and describe what the construction does here before saying what it can do in
general. Both are set out in `core-method.md` §3.

## 5. Genre expectations

Use to set expectations in the global read, and to choose which phenomena are likely present.

| Genre | Typical architecture | Where the learning value sits |
|---|---|---|
| News report | inverted pyramid; quote-attribution chains | attribution verbs, nominalisation, hedging through sourcing |
| Op-ed / column | counter-intuitive hook → concession → claim → implication | concession architecture, stance markers, colon-thesis |
| Academic abstract / paper | gap → method → finding → limit | nominalisation, scope narrowing, hedging, passive-with-agent-hiding |
| Popular science | concrete hook → mechanism → caveat | analogy structure, information unpacking, register mixing |
| Personal essay | scene → reflection → return | tense shifting, participles, rhythm, concrete-abstract alternation |
| Literary fiction | scene and voice | free indirect discourse, sentence rhythm, punctuation, ambiguity |
| Business / report | summary → drivers → risks | nominalisation, modality, conditional framing of risk |
| Marketing copy | benefit → proof → call to action | second person, imperatives, evaluative adjectives, ellipsis |

If the genre is not on this list, describe its architecture from the text rather than forcing a row.

## 6. What not to spend time on in English

- **Spelling and pronunciation.** Not close reading.
- **Irregular verb tables, article rules in general.** Only when a specific sentence turns on one.
- **Phrasal verb lists.** Only phrasal verbs whose meaning is genuinely non-transparent *in this
  context*.
- **Word formation / suffix rules.** Unless the nominalisation is doing argumentative work.
- **"Advanced" synonyms.** Substituting *utilise* for *use* is not progress.
- **British vs American spelling.** Irrelevant to reading comprehension.
- **Grammar terminology for its own sake.** If the sentence can be explained without the term, drop
  the term.
